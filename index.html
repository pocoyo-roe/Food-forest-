Forest gardening for British gardens

Plant a guild, not just a tree.

A tree guild is a fruit tree with a ring of companions that feed the soil, cover the ground, bring in pollinators and give you a second, third and fourth harvest from the same patch. Here are nine guilds that work in the UK climate, with what to pick when, when to prune, and what to expect in year three versus year ten.

          Before you plant

          Preparing the ground

          Five ways to clear grass and weeds before you plant.

          Grass is the biggest competitor of a young fruit tree, and perennial weeds will outlast anything you plant if they get a head start. Every method below aims for the same result: weed-free, covered soil you never have to dig again. They differ in how fast you can plant, how much material you need and how they treat the life already in the soil.

            How much material?

            A bulk bag holds roughly 0.7–1 m³. Tree surgeons often give away fresh wood chip, which is ideal around trees but too coarse to sow seed into.

            Cardboard and compost (no dig)

            Charles Dowding, Somerset · the method the RHS describes for new no-dig beds

            Plant: Same dayEffort: 2/5Cost: HighPerennial weeds: Weakens couch
            1. Clear brambles and woody stems. Cut grass and weeds to the ground and leave them there.
            2. Lay plain brown cardboard with tape and staples removed, overlapping each sheet by 15 cm so nothing pushes through the joins.
            3. Spread 10–15 cm of compost or well-rotted manure on top. Dowding often skips the cardboard on beds with this much compost and uses it only on paths.
            4. Plant straight away into the compost. Cut a cross in the cardboard where the tree goes.
            5. Pull the few weeds that come through in the first months. Add about 5 cm of compost each year after.
            Plant
            Same day
            Effort
            moving compost
            Cost
            High: 100–150 litres of compost per m²
            Perennial weeds

            Soil life
            Undisturbed, fed from the top

            Dowding has compared dug and undug beds since 2007. Over 2007–2022 the no-dig beds gave 1,471 kg against 1,327 kg for the dug beds, and out-yielded them in 14 of 16 years. It is one grower's paired-bed trial, not replicated research. Dowding: 13 years of dig vs no dig · RHS: no-dig gardening

            Sheet mulching with support species

            Geoff Lawton, Permaculture Research Institute · the permaculture "instant garden"

            Plant: Trees now; the mulch takes 4–6 months to break downEffort: 3/5Cost: Low if you find free straw, leaves or chipPerennial weeds: Fair
            1. Scatter manure or compost straight onto the cut grass to feed the worms.
            2. Cover with overlapping cardboard or thick newspaper and soak it.
            3. Pile on a thick layer of straw, hay, leaves or wood chip. Thicker is better; a thin layer lets grass through.
            4. Plant trees through holes cut in the cardboard. Put small plants and seeds into pockets of compost.
            5. Plant far more nitrogen-fixing support plants than fruit trees at the start, then cut them back each year and drop the prunings as mulch.
            Plant

            Lawton's teaching that a new food forest starts as mostly support species (often quoted as about 90%) reaches most people through course summaries rather than a written source, so treat the exact figure loosely. Cutting nitrogen fixers also makes them shed roots, which feeds the soil. Summary of Lawton's Establishing a Food Forest · Pip Magazine: grow your own mulch

            Wood chip cover (Back to Eden)

            Paul Gautschi, Washington State · made famous by the 2011 film Back to Eden

            Plant: Trees now; sowing is easier after a yearEffort: 3/5Cost: Often free from tree surgeonsPerennial weeds: Fair
            1. Lay newspaper or cardboard over the grass without weeding first.
            2. Add 7–10 cm of compost where you will sow or plant small plants.
            3. Cover everything with 7–10 cm of wood chip. Gautschi uses fresh arborist chip around trees and older, composted chip where he sows.
            4. Never dig the chip in. Pull it aside to plant, then let it settle back.
            5. Top up as it rots. Fallen leaves can do the job in later years.

Research on wood chip mulch does support the key rules: chips on the surface only take nitrogen from the thin layer where chip meets soil, so don't mix them in or sow small seeds directly into fresh chip.             A light-proof cover for a season

            "Occultation" with silage tarps, used by no-till market gardeners · Martin Crawford's year under mulch

            Plant: After 3 weeks to a full yearEffort: 1/5Cost: One reusable sheetPerennial weeds: Good if left on a whole season
            1. Cut the grass short and water it if the ground is dry.
            2. Cover with black silage plastic, old carpet underlay or another light-proof sheet. Weigh the edges down with sandbags or bury them.
            3. Leave it on through the growing season. Annual weeds and green manures die in 3–4 weeks of warm weather; turf and perennial weeds take months.
            4. Lift the cover and plant straight into the dead turf. Mulch at once so the soil isn't left bare.
            5. Reuse the sheet on the next patch.

            In an Iowa farm trial, three to four weeks of tarping killed a rye cover crop and set weeds back; one week was not enough. Martin Crawford clears ground under plastic or cardboard for about a year before planting ground covers. Slugs shelter under sheets, so check before planting.             Dig or rotovate once, then sow a ground cover

            Conventional cultivation used once, then never again · RHS green manure guidance

            Plant: Trees now; cover crop in 6–8 weeksEffort: 4/5Cost: LowPerennial weeds: Poor
            1. Only on ground without bindweed or couch, or after forking their roots out by hand. A rotovator chops their roots into pieces that each regrow.
            2. Dig, broadfork or rotovate once to break up compaction or a plough pan. Remove turf first, or stack it upside down to rot into loam.
            3. Rake level and sow a ground cover within days, before weeds take the bare soil. White clover with phacelia from spring to late summer, or grazing rye from August to November.
            4. Plant the tree and shrubs into the green cover, cutting it back around each one and leaving the cuttings as mulch.
            5. Never cultivate again. Let the clover stay as a living mulch, or cut the green manure down in spring and plant into it.
            Plant
            Trees now; cover crop in 6–8 weeks
            Effort
            heavy, but once only
            Cost
            Low: seed is 1–2 g per m² for clover
            Perennial weeds
            Poor: spreads bindweed and couch
            Soil life
            Set back, then recovers under permanent cover

            Useful on compacted, bare or builder's soil where no mulch method can break the hard layer. Cultivation does cost soil life: in Rothamsted's survey of English farm fields, tillage reduced earthworms and 42% of fields showed signs of being over-worked.             Which cardboard is safe to use?

            Use plain brown corrugated boxes with tape and labels removed. Avoid glossy, waxed, printed or grease-proof card: a 2024 study found PFAS ("forever chemicals") in cardboard used as poultry bedding, and coated food packaging is the likeliest source. Plain boxes are thought to be low risk. University of Maine Extension on cardboard and PFAS · National Allotment Society: using cardboard

            Under your feet

            Soil life

            The life under your feet and what current research says.

            A fruit tree feeds the soil as much as the soil feeds it. Roots leak sugars that feed bacteria and fungi; fungi reach far beyond the roots and trade water and phosphorus for those sugars; tiny predators eat the microbes and release nitrogen right where the roots are. Hover or tap the cross-section to meet them.

            How soil builds
            1. Leaves capture carbon

              Photosynthesis turns air and water into sugars. A share goes down to the roots.

            2. Roots feed the microbes

              Root tips release sugars, acids and proteins, and shed cells. Bacteria and fungi crowd into this zone, the rhizosphere.

            3. Fungi trade and reach

              Mycorrhizal fungi wrap or enter roots and spread threads through the soil, swapping phosphorus, nitrogen and water for plant sugar.

            4. Predators release nutrients

              Protozoa and nematodes eat bacteria and fungi and excrete the nitrogen they don't need as plant-available ammonium.

            5. Dead microbes become soil

              Microbial remains stick to clay and silt and can make up more than half of soil organic carbon. Fungal threads, worm casts and microbial glues bind crumbs that hold air and water.

            Current research:
            Well supported

            Most species live in soil

            A 2023 review estimated that about 59% of all species on Earth live in soil (give or take 15%), double the 2006 estimate. Fungi are around 90% soil-dwelling.

            Anthony, Bender and van der Heijden 2023, PNAS

            Well supported

            Digging costs soil life

            A meta-analysis of 54 field studies found that less tillage and winter cover crops each raised mycorrhizal colonisation of crop roots by about 30%. Rothamsted's farm survey found tillage cut earthworm numbers, and adding organic matter did not make up for it.

            Bowles et al. 2017, tillage and cover crop meta-analysis · Stroud 2019, PLOS ONE

            Emerging

            Plants pour carbon into fungi

            A 2023 meta-analysis of 194 datasets estimated that land plants pass about 13 gigatonnes of CO₂-equivalent a year to mycorrhizal fungi, roughly a third of yearly fossil fuel emissions. It is a flow, not a store: how long that carbon stays in soil is still unknown.

            Hawkins et al. 2023, Current Biology · University of Sheffield summary

            Emerging

            Dead microbes build lasting carbon

            The "microbial carbon pump": microbes eat plant inputs, grow, die, and their remains bind to minerals. Biomarker studies suggest this necromass can be over half of soil organic carbon, so feeding living microbes matters as much as adding bulky organic matter.

            Liang, Amelung and Lehmann 2019, Global Change Biology · Kästner et al. 2021 review

            Emerging

            Mapping the underground

            In 2025 the Society for the Protection of Underground Networks published the first global maps of mycorrhizal fungal diversity from 2.8 billion DNA sequences. Over 90% of the richest hotspots lie outside protected areas, though only a tiny fraction of the Earth has been sampled.

            SPUN: the Underground Atlas · Mongabay report

            Emerging

            Roots that farm microbes

            The "rhizophagy cycle" proposes that root tips take in bacteria, strip nutrients from them with oxidative chemistry, and release the survivors from root hairs. It has been seen in many plant species, but how much it matters for plant nutrition is not yet known.

            White et al. 2018, Microorganisms

            Contested

            The "wood wide web"

            Fungal threads can link the roots of different trees. But a 2023 review found weak evidence that these networks are widespread, or that they move enough resources to help seedlings, and no peer-reviewed evidence that "mother trees" send resources to their offspring. Unsupported claims in the literature had doubled over 25 years.

            Karst, Jones and Hoeksema 2023, Nature Ecology & Evolution

            Practitioner idea

            The soil food web

            Elaine Ingham's framework, taught widely to growers, groups soil life into bacteria, fungi, protozoa, nematodes and arthropods that cycle nutrients between them. The basics are textbook soil ecology; her commercial claims about brewed compost teas are less well tested.

            USDA Soil Biology Primer (Ingham et al.)

            Practitioner idea

            The liquid carbon pathway

            Soil ecologist Christine Jones argues that sugars flowing from living roots, not rotting plant matter, build most new topsoil, so diverse plants kept growing for as long as possible matter most. The idea fits the research above, but her specific figures haven't been tested in peer-reviewed studies.

            Jones, interview in Acres USA (PDF)

            Keep roots growing all year

            Living roots are the main food supply for soil life. Mix evergreens, winter ground covers and early bulbs so something is always photosynthesising.

            Never leave soil bare

            Bare soil dries, crusts and loses its fungi. Mulch, ground cover or a green manure should cover every patch.

            Feed from the top

            Lay compost, leaves and chop-and-drop on the surface and let worms take it down. Dig only once, if at all.

            Woody mulch for trees

            Fruit trees grow best in fungal soil like a woodland edge. Wood chip and fallen leaves feed fungi; keep it 10 cm clear of the trunk.

            Diversity below follows diversity above

            Each plant feeds a different set of microbes. A guild of ten species supports far more soil life than a tree in mown grass.

            Go easy on fertiliser

            Plants supplied with soluble nutrients send less sugar to their fungal partners. Comfrey, nitrogen fixers and compost feed the soil without cutting the fungi out.

            Before you dig

            UK growing notes

            Planting, pruning, frost and pollination basics.

            Plant bare-root in winter

            Bare-root trees and bushes are cheaper and establish better. Plant from November to March when the ground isn't frozen or waterlogged. Pot-grown plants can go in any time but need watering through their first summer.

            Prune stone fruit in summer

            Plums, damsons and cherries get silver leaf and bacterial canker through winter cuts. Prune them on a dry day between June and August. Apples, pears, quince and medlar are pruned in winter while dormant.

            Watch for late frosts

            Frost in April and May kills blossom. Avoid planting early-flowering pears and plums at the bottom of slopes, where cold air pools. Medlar and quince flower late and cope with frost pockets better.

            Check pollination groups

            Most apples and pears need a partner of a different variety flowering at the same time, within about 20 m. Nurseries list varieties by flowering group (1 to 7). A neighbour's tree or a crab apple often does the job.

            Mulch, then plant into it

            Kill the grass with cardboard and 10 cm of woodchip a season before planting. Grass is the main competitor of young trees. Top up the woodchip each winter; the fungi it feeds are what woodland soils run on.

            Plan for shade to arrive

            In years one to four the guild is sunny and the understory produces most of the food. As the canopy closes, swap sun-lovers like strawberries and lavender for shade-tolerant currants, wild garlic and sweet cicely.

            Animals in the system

            Chickens, soil and compost

            How hens can turn waste and weeds into compost and fertile ground, and the UK rules to know first.

            Hens scratch, shred and manure whatever you give them. Kept moving and given plenty of carbon to work, they turn bedding, garden waste and weeds into compost, clear ground for new guilds and clean up windfalls under trees. Left too long in one place they strip the ground bare, compact it and overload it with nitrogen. Every method below works by managing where the hens are and what they scratch through.

            UK rules before you start

            • Register your birds. Since 2024 every keeper in Great Britain must register with the Animal and Plant Health Agency, even for one hen. New keepers have 30 days. Government response
            • No kitchen scraps. It is illegal to feed catering waste or kitchen scraps to poultry, even pets, because of the risk of spreading animal diseases. Vegetables picked from the garden and fed straight to the hens are treated differently from food that has been through the kitchen. This rules out the food-scrap compost systems used in the US unless you change the inputs. APHA warning · Why the law exists
            • Bird flu. An Avian Influenza Prevention Zone requires biosecurity from all keepers, and housing orders can require birds to be kept under cover. Plan a covered run you can close. Current bird flu guidance
            • Rats and foxes. Shut hens in at night and use a rat-proof feeder. Compost runs with food in them attract rats fast.
            Five ways to put hens to work

            Compost-turning flock ("chicken tractor on steroids")

            Geoff Lawton, Zaytuna Farm, New South Wales

            Scale: large gardenLabour: weekly pile buildingOutput: about 1 m³ of compost a weekUK: no kitchen scraps
            1. Lay bedding under the roost and in the nest boxes. After a week it is full of manure.
            2. Fork the manured bedding into a round compost cage with other bulky material: hay, straw, other manures and green waste.
            3. Let the hens onto the pile. They scratch, shred and turn it while eating insects and seeds, and the pile heats up.
            4. Build a new pile each week inside a ring of moveable electric netting, and move the old ones on as they mature.
            5. Lawton reports rich compost in about 30 days, with the hens feeding largely from the piles rather than bought grain.
            Soil benefit
            A steady supply of finished compost for guilds and beds
            Feed
            Lawton gives grit and little or no grain; in the UK you will need some layers pellets
            UK change
            Build piles from garden waste, bedding and bought-in manure only. Feeding kitchen or restaurant scraps to poultry is illegal here.

            Lawton's full video sits behind a login, so the details reach most people through summaries. One forum write-up of it says the compost-fed hens laid as well as grain-fed ones, and often better; treat that as second-hand. ECHO in Florida adapted the system after Lawton taught it on a design course. Summary of Lawton's video · Hobby Farms on chickens and compost · ECHO's adaptation

            Chicken tractor to prepare new ground

            A moveable pen over beds or new planting areas · used on a large scale at Zaytuna with electric netting

            Scale: any gardenLabour: move every 1–3 daysSoil benefit: clears and manures groundUK: good fit
            1. Put a bottomless pen over grass or weeds where a new guild or bed will go.
            2. Add a thin layer of compost or straw for the hens to work, and move the pen along every couple of days, before the ground turns bare and compacted.
            3. The hens eat the weeds and grubs, scratch the surface open and leave manure behind.
            4. Cover the worked strip straight away with wood chip or compost so the soil is never left bare.
            5. Plant trees and shrubs into it. Give the ground a season under mulch before sowing salads and other crops eaten raw, so the fresh manure can break down.
            Soil benefit
            Kills turf and adds manure without digging, an alternative to cardboard for ground preparation
            Watch out
            Left too long in one place, hens strip the ground bare, compact it and overload it with nitrogen
            UK change
            None needed; a small pen of three or four hens suits most gardens

            One gardener's version uses long beds about a metre wide, adding compost from one end and moving the tractor along every two days. Compare this with the other methods under Preparing the ground. No-work chicken gardening

            Compost run (the Edible Acres system)

            Sean Dembrosky, Edible Acres, Trumansburg, New York · modelled on the Vermont Compost Company

            Scale: small farmLabour: collecting materials, some hand turningBonus: compost heatUK: adapt the inputs
            1. Fence a run and fill it deep with a bulky mix of hay, wood chip and food waste.
            2. Let the flock live on it. They forage through the pile all day, turning it as they go.
            3. Keep adding fresh material on top; take finished compost from the bottom or the oldest end.
            4. Supplement the hens with grain. Edible Acres trades for waste whole grains and sprouts leftover seed.
            5. The working pile stays warm, and Edible Acres has used it to heat a winter run and a polytunnel.
            Soil benefit
            Large volumes of compost for a nursery and forest garden
            Feed
            Grain is the main cost; the pile supplies insects and greens
            UK change
            Feed the pile with wood chip, leaves, straw, grass clippings, garden waste and weeds, not kitchen or food-business scraps

            Most of the detail comes from Dembrosky's own videos and forum posts rather than a written guide. The Vermont Compost Company, which inspired it, has fed about 800 hens on community food scraps with no bought grain since 1998. A visit to Edible Acres · Dembrosky on the system · Vermont Compost Company

            Deep litter coop and run

            The standard small-flock method, used by backyard keepers everywhere

            Scale: any flockLabour: top up, empty once a yearSoil benefit: compost made in placeUK: good fit
            1. Start with 10–20 cm of dry carbon bedding: wood shavings, straw, hemp or, outside, wood chip.
            2. Add a fresh layer whenever it smells or looks wet, rather than mucking out.
            3. The hens turn the manure into the bedding as they scratch, and it composts slowly in place.
            4. Once a year, dig out the bottom layer. Finish it in a compost heap or spread it as mulch around established trees.
            Soil benefit
            Rich, part-composted litter for mulching guilds
            Watch out
            Keep it dry and well ventilated; stop if it smells of ammonia
            UK change
            Have a covered area ready in case of a bird flu housing order

            Carbon bedding soaks up the nitrogen in manure; moisture and air keep it composting rather than going sour. Composting slows almost to a stop in a hard freeze. The deep litter method

            Hens following other animals or the harvest

            Joel Salatin's Eggmobile at Polyface Farm, Virginia · orchard trials in Oregon and Michigan

            Scale: orchard or pastureLabour: moving a mobile houseSoil benefit: spreads manure thinlyUK: for larger sites
            1. At Polyface, cattle graze a fresh strip each day and a mobile hen house follows about three days behind.
            2. The hens scratch apart the cow pats, spreading the manure and eating the fly larvae in them.
            3. Moving them on keeps their own high-nitrogen manure from building up in one place.
            4. In an orchard, let hens in after harvest and in early spring to clean up windfalls, weeds and grubs, then move them on.
            Soil benefit
            Manure spread thinly and evenly instead of concentrated near the house
            Pests
            Mixed evidence; see the research below
            UK change
            Fits a field-scale food forest or orchard more than a garden

            Salatin's rule is that portable housing stops nitrogen and disease building up where birds gather. The Polyface Eggmobile · Oregon State: chicken tractors in orchards

            What the research says
            Emerging

            Compost-fed hens can lay as well as grain-fed

            A Vermont farm trial compared hens fed from an active food-scrap compost system with grain-fed hens: 1,032 eggs against 1,036 over 107 days, and deeper yolks (13 against 5–7 on the colour scale). The compost system took more labour and needed wood chip and hay. Food scraps cannot legally be used this way in the UK.

            SARE report FNE14-801, Black Dirt Farm · Operator's manual (PDF)

            Mixed

            Density decides whether hens build or damage soil

            In an Italian olive grove grazed by chickens for over 20 years, soil carbon and nitrogen were lower near the houses where birds crowded, and matched ungrazed soil 50 m away. The authors conclude that high density harms soil while moderate grazing lets it recover.

            University of Perugia, olive grove study (2025)

            Emerging

            Manure raises organic matter where it lands

            A SARE pastured poultry project found higher organic matter and better water infiltration where manure was deposited, but no change in soil compaction after grazing. A 2025 North Carolina silvopasture study found more soil carbon under rotated broilers, though some of the difference existed before the birds arrived.

            SARE: pastured poultry project · UNC Asheville silvopasture study

            Mixed

            Chickens in orchards: good on weeds, patchy on pests

            In a 13-month Oregon trial, chickens (especially broilers) reduced weeds and some pests in an organic apple orchard, but results were "not as promising" as hoped. Michigan trials in 1990–91 found spring flocks controlled weeds as well as herbicide, with no clear effect on codling moth.

            Oregon State EM 9320 · SARE: sustainable apple production, Michigan

            Well supported

            Trees get hens out ranging

            Woodland Trust research on UK egg farms found that planting trees on around 20% of a hen range encourages birds to range further, which reduces feather pecking and spreads manure, and creates woodland-edge habitat for song thrushes, tree sparrows and bats.

            Woodland Trust research briefing (PDF) · Tree planting for poultry (PDF)

            Keep them off new guilds

            Hens dig around young trees and undermine their roots. Keep them out for the first two years, or ring trees with logs, flat stones or brush so they can't scratch near the trunk.

            Use them before you plant

            Run a chicken tractor over the site of your next guild for a few weeks, then mulch it. It does the job of cardboard and adds manure.

            Bring them in after harvest

            A short visit to mature guilds in autumn and early spring cleans up windfalls, slugs and grubs, then move them on before they reach the mulch around shallow roots.

            Plant for the flock

            Mulberry, elder, sea buckthorn and Siberian pea shrub drop fruit and seed hens love, and give them shade and shelter from hawks.

            More carbon than you think

            Every bucket of manure needs several of wood chip, straw or leaves. If the run or the heap smells of ammonia, add more carbon.

            Compost before it feeds crops

            Fresh chicken manure is strong enough to scorch plants. Compost it, or let it break down under mulch for a season, before it goes near salads and root crops.

            The research on chickens and soil is thin: mostly short trials, single farms and practitioner accounts. The pattern across it is consistent, though. Moving hens and giving them carbon builds soil; crowding them in one place wears it out.

            About the figures on this page

            Tree sizes, pruning times and pollination advice follow the RHS pages above. There is no authoritative UK table of yields per tree by rootstock, so the yield ranges are estimates from typical nursery figures and should be read as a rough guide. Planting densities are typical spacings for each plant, not from a single source.

            The UK forest garden studies above suggest forest gardens give less food per square metre than an intensive kitchen garden, but for much less work, and that yields keep rising as the trees mature.

            Yields are typical fresh-weight ranges for UK gardens with reasonable care. Good years can double them; a frosty April can halve them. Treat them as a planning guide, not a promise.

            "Young guild" means years 3–4 after planting. "Mature guild" means year 8 onwards (year 12 onwards for mulberry).
